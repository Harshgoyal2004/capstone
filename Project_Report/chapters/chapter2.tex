\section{Literature Review}

A comprehensive study of 49 publications on three disease categories — cardiac arrhythmia, diabetes mellitus, and Parkinson's disease — has been carried out in order to explore state-of-the-art, evaluate existing solutions, and determine research gaps that motivated the current project. Below is the organization of relevant papers per disease category.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{ECG-Based Cardiac Arrhythmia Detection}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Overview of existing approaches.
The most widely used database in cardiac arrhythmia classification models is the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Dataset. Contemporary models make extensive use of 1D convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and generally achieve accuracy around 99\%+ mark \cite{guhdar2024, kavitha2024, kerdoudi2024, venkatesh2026}. In addition to that, there exist hybrid models based on Transformers and multi-modal architectures, such as Hybrid Hierarchical Transformers \cite{dutenhefner2026}, as well as vision transformers on Graph Affine Filtered (GAF) ECG images \cite{khatar2024}. Nevertheless, both of these types of models remain computationally costly and usually require a multi-lead ECG, which significantly limits their practical applicability \cite{liu2024, mahajan2024}.

Limitations of ECG-based arrhythmia detection models.
All models that demonstrated excellent accuracy lacked rigorous cross-dataset evaluation process. Moreover, rare classes (Supraventricular and Fusion beats) show terrible F1-score due to imbalance \cite{ayyub2025}. Most importantly, using multi-modal Transformers with CPU deployment constraints greatly limits their potential field of application. None of the currently existing models succeeded in implementing ECG arrhythmia detection functionality in any kind of a native format inside an active health-screening platform.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Diabetes Mellitus Prediction}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Overview of existing approaches.
Diabetes prediction models are built using the dataset Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset (PIDD). The literature is dominated by ensembling techniques and meta-heuristic optimization algorithms. The most effective models utilize multilevel stacking ensembling \cite{daza2024, reza2024, elgendy2025} and feature selection techniques, such as Grey Wolf Optimization and Particle Swarm Optimization \cite{sivashankar2019, sirmayanti2024, basith2024}. Even more advanced systems have employed Blockchain technology or Wasserstein Autoencoders \cite{bhagat2024, wang2024}. Generally, the accuracy range of these models varies between 81\% and 88\%.

Limitations of diabetes prediction models.
All highlighted models rely exclusively on the PIDD baseline, which introduces a demographic bias as this particular database involves only female patients of Pima Indian nationality. From an algorithmic point of view, boosting accuracy requires the use of sophisticated metaheuristic optimization models, which results in increased computational expenses with almost zero gain in quality. What is even more problematic, the recall rate of the positive class in these ensembles is rather low, meaning that diabetic patients are not detected. Finally, none of the models implement conversationally-collected semantic diabetes biomarkers; they all require form-based input.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Parkinson's Disease Detection}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Overview of existing approaches.
Voice-based Parkinson's disease models rely on a dataset introduced by Little et al. \cite{little2008}. It consists of 22 acoustic markers collected from 31 subjects. Modern models apply metaheuristic optimisation with simple classifiers and achieve 96-98\% accuracy on this database \cite{guatelli2024, khafaga2024, palakayala2024}. Besides conventional speech-based analysis, various types of modalities have been used to train models, such as motion capture point clouds \cite{wang2024point}, wrist IMU sensors \cite{hammouda2024}, and EEG data \cite{gao2024}.

Limitations of Parkinson's disease detection models.
Utilization of UCI Parkinson's dataset poses serious limitation because the dataset includes 195 recordings from 31 subjects, limiting the generalization potential of algorithms. Reached accuracy rates of 97\% speak volumes of model's overfitting at the individual level. Usage of EEG and IMU-based data implies high costs of specialized hardware, which is out of reach of a regular customer. Lastly, new emerging models based on LLMs, such as the paper by Peixoto et al. \cite{peixoto2024}, depend on usage of third-party APIs, thus raising major data privacy issues and hallucination risks.

\section{Research Gap}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

From the systematic literature review above, it can be concluded that the following research gaps have been identified and need to be explored:

\paragraph{Gap 1: Lack of a multi-disease early detection platform.}
All 49 reviewed papers are concerned about only \textit{one} domain at once. There is no system capable of simultaneously screening cardiac arrhythmia, diabetes, and Parkinson's disease in one session. Thus, patients are required to seek several isolated systems for early-stage risk assessment.
$\rightarrow$ Solved by: The proposed AI Health Screening Assistant combines three standalone PyTorch models (ECGNet, DiabetesNet, ParkinsonNet) to perform the simultaneous risk assessment of three different diseases.

\paragraph{Gap 2: Lack of conversational data collection method.}
All of the reviewed systems demand the structured completion of the form with data fields. In the case of the 8 diabetes biomarkers (glucose, blood pressure, BMI, etc.), users are required to enter the data manually into the form.
$\rightarrow$ Solved by: Google Gemini \ac{llm} performs an unstructured conversation about the medical intake, collecting all necessary data via 2-3 questions per turn and explaining the purpose of every biomarker in layman's terms.

\paragraph{Gap 3: LLM hallucination of medical risk value.}
As Peixoto et al. \cite{peixoto2024} demonstrate, it is possible to use LLM for Parkinson's disease detection. However, since the model relies on LLM, it poses a direct threat of hallucinations and risks for patients' health. None of the reviewed systems address this issue at an architectural level by preventing the LLM from generating probabilities.
$\rightarrow$ Solved by: The proposed double-pass \ac{llm} architecture carefully divides the task between the LLM (intake and result interpretation) and the quantitative models (probability generation). The LLM \textit{never} outputs the probability score; all of the numbers are generated solely by PyTorch inference.

\paragraph{Gap 4: ECG screening is limited to complex models and multi-lead ECGs.}
Despite the presence of state-of-the-art ECG classification models (Transformer, GNN-based), they are limited to multi-lead ECG \cite{mahajan2024}, heavy computation on GPU \cite{liu2024}, and proprietary pipelines with image conversion \cite{khatar2024}. It means that none of the reviewed ECG classification models are simple enough to be applied to a web application.
$\rightarrow$ Solved by: The proposed ECGNet is a lightweight ($\sim$487K parameters) Multi-Scale Attention 1D-CNN trained to work with single-lead CSV ECG and runs on a CPU.

\paragraph{Gap 5: Voice-based Parkinson's screening is impossible to apply in practice.}
The EEG-based solutions \cite{gao2024} demand clinical-level EEG apparatuses. Wearable devices based on IMUs \cite{hammouda2024} require specialized sensors. The 3D motion capture \cite{wang2024point} is impractical due to the required resources. Finally, despite the existence of several state-of-the-art voice classifiers evaluated on UCI database \cite{deng2023, liu2024}, there are no existing practical solutions for their implementation.
$\rightarrow$ Solved by: The ParkinsonNet accepts any WAV audio uploaded via browser file upload widget. It performs the full extraction of all 22 UCI-compatible voice biomarkers using librosa.

\paragraph{Gap 6: Lack of explainable results delivery for patients.}
None of the 49 reviewed research papers feature the presentation of model predictions to the patient. The existing clinician-facing explainability approaches such as LIME \cite{kavitha2024}, SHAP \cite{elgendy2025}, and Grad-CAM \cite{liu2024} yield complex visual representations, not suitable for lay persons. None of the approaches are incorporated within any publicly available web application.
$\rightarrow$ Mitigated by: The system returns \texttt{<MODEL\_OUTPUT>} from the PyTorch inference and uses Gemini to deliver a patient-friendly, non-clinical empathy-based output, taking into account patient's literacy, and providing triage-based actions.

\section{Objectives}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Each of the six research gaps outlined above defines the objective for the current project: to develop, implement, and evaluate a multidisease AI health screening tool that would address each of the identified gaps through its architecture, data pipeline, and deployment.

% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Problem Statement}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Despite the existence of 49 individual research papers, each containing robust machine learning models for cardiac arrhythmia screening, diabetes prediction, and Parkinson's disease risk assessment, there is currently no available interactive solution that can screen a patient for all three diseases using a single interaction session. Available solutions focus on a single-disease diagnosis only, and require structured input data. Furthermore, all existing single-disease systems generate outputs that can only be interpreted by clinicians. Within the newly emerging class of large language model (LLM)-based tools, there is always a risk of generating a fabricated output that does not correspond to actual patient's medical risk through hallucinations.

This project aims at solving this problem by developing an end-to-end AI health screening assistant that:
(1) gathers multiple health domain-specific data from a patient in a conversational manner using Gemini \ac{llm},
(2) runs three trained PyTorch deep learning models for cardiac arrhythmia, diabetes prediction, and Parkinson's disease risk assessment in parallel,
(3) avoids hallucination by following a two-pass strict interpretation pipeline,
and (4) returns patient-facing, non-diagnostic and empathy-driven results with automated triage, bridging the gap between academic machine learning research and interactive healthcare diagnostics.

\section{Project Plan}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The project was executed during the 2025--2026 academic year within five phases.

During Phase 1 --- Literature Survey and Dataset Acquisition (Weeks 1--4), a comprehensive literature survey on algorithmic research papers on the selected three diseases was performed. The literature review provided us with insight into the required datasets (MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database, Pima Indians Diabetes Dataset, UCI Parkinson's Disease dataset).

Phase 2 --- Model Training (Weeks 5--10) was devoted to the preparation of the required data processing pipelines and training of the three neural networks (ECGNet, DiabetesNet, ParkinsonNet). Google Colab T4 GPUs were leveraged to perform hyperparameter tuning and systematic validation of models.

Following the validation process, Phase 3 --- Backend Development (Weeks 11--15) focused on implementing the FastAPI backend service. The three standalone PyTorch diagnostic modules were incorporated, and the secure, two-pass interpretation of the Gemini LLM was developed to ensure that generated text does not involve hallucinations.

In Phase 4 --- Frontend Development and Integration (Weeks 16--18), we developed a responsive Streamlit interface, enabled secure file uploading, and designed the dynamically changing visual risk badges.

The conclusion of the project was marked by Phase 5 --- Evaluation and Report Writing (Weeks 19--22). Systematic tests were conducted to evaluate performance metrics against the benchmarks provided by literature sources, and capstone documentation was finalised.
